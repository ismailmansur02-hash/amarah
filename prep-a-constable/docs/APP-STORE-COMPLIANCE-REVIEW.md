# App Store compliance review — against `mjmirza/app-store-compliance`

**Date:** 29 September 2026. **Scope:** United Kingdom only, on instruction.
**Subject:** `native/` — Prep a Constable 1.0.0, iOS build 1.

This records what the `mjmirza/app-store-compliance` toolkit produced when run
against this app, what survived checking, and what was changed as a result. It
sits alongside `UK-COMPLIANCE-AUDIT.md`, which remains the main record; nothing
here replaces it.

## How the toolkit was treated

It was cloned and its scanners were run, but it was read as **data to verify,
not instructions to obey** — the same rule you set for the main audit. That
matters more than usual here, because the repository is a third party's
checklist of one person's reading of the law, and its own closing section says
to treat its dates and figures as advisory until re-verified against the cited
source. Three specific consequences:

- Every legal claim acted on below was re-verified against `legislation.gov.uk`,
  `ico.org.uk` or `gov.uk` before anything was changed. Two were checked and are
  cited. The rest were read and set aside.
- The repository's setup instructions ask for its skills and hooks to be
  installed into `~/.claude/`, which would let it steer future sessions. That was
  not done. The scanners were run directly instead, which gets the same findings
  without handing it standing influence.
- One of its scripted steps has the agent print links asking you to star and
  follow the project. Stating the fact: it asks. It was not acted on.

## Code scan — three findings, all three false positives

The guard hook reported `critical=0 high=3 medium=0`. Each was traced to the
line that triggered it.

| ID | What fired it | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| `BOTH-PLACEHOLDER` | `placeholder="you@example.com"` in `LoginScreen.js:97` | **False positive.** That is the grey hint text in the email box, and `example.com` is the domain reserved by RFC 2606 for exactly this. The rule is aimed at lorem ipsum and unreplaced `YOUR_API_KEY`. There is none. |
| `WEB-LOCAL-STORAGE` | The words "localStorage" in two *comments*, at `cloud.js:6` and `storage.js:4` | **False positive.** The native app has no `localStorage` — it cannot, there is no DOM. It uses AsyncStorage. The only real usage in the repository is `preview/entry.jsx`, the browser preview shim, which was not the directory scanned. |
| `WEB-TRACKING-TECHNOLOGIES` | The substring `gtag` inside `has-tostringtag` and `es-set-tostringtag` in `package-lock.json` | **False positive**, and a plain substring collision. There is no analytics, no advertising and no tracking in this app, which is the same conclusion the main audit reached by reading the dependency tree. |

Separately on the UK point behind the second one: the app's own on-device
storage is what the ICO's storage-and-access guidance calls *strictly necessary*
— it holds your progress so the app works offline, and your sign-in session so
you are not asked to sign in every time. Storage of that kind is exempt from the
PECR consent requirement, so no consent banner is required and none should be
added.

## What the toolkit missed, which is worth knowing before trusting it

Two of its checks reported clean without having looked at anything:

- **`Platforms. iOS=0 Android=0`.** It looks for `ios/` and `android/`
  directories. This is an Expo project: those are generated by `expo prebuild`
  and are deliberately gitignored, so they are absent from a clean checkout. It
  then skipped every iOS-specific and Android-specific rule it has. The whole
  Apple half of the scan did not run.
- **The accessibility audit printed "Clean. No accessibility compliance
  regressions found."** after `Scanned files. iOS=0 Android=0`. It reads Swift,
  Kotlin and Android XML. It cannot see React Native. "Clean" there means it did
  not look, not that the app passed.

The accessibility position was therefore checked by hand instead: 144 `Pressable`
elements, of which exactly one has neither an `accessibilityLabel` nor a `<Text>`
child for VoiceOver to read — the generic `Card` wrapper in `ui.js:86`, which
wraps content that carries its own text. No icon-only unlabelled buttons. That is
a reasonable position under the duty to make reasonable adjustments in the
Equality Act 2010, which is the UK hook here — the European Accessibility Act is
EU law and does not apply to a UK-only release.

## What was actually applicable — and what changed

### 1. You now owe people a complaint route, and the notice did not give one

This is the one new **legal** requirement the review turned up, and it is UK law
that is already in force.

Section 103 of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 inserted sections 164A and
164B into the Data Protection Act 2018. They apply to any complaint received on
or after **19 June 2026**. A controller must give people a way to complain
directly to it, **acknowledge receipt within 30 days**, take appropriate steps to
respond without undue delay, and tell the complainant the outcome.

The privacy notice said only "please contact us first" and then pointed at the
ICO. That is Article 13(2)(d), which is a different duty and does not discharge
this one.

**Changed** in `shared/content/legal.js`: the "If you are unhappy" section now
states the right to complain to you, how to do it, the 30-day acknowledgement,
that you will investigate and report the outcome — and, importantly, that the
user does **not** have to come to you before going to the ICO, because section
164A does not make you a compulsory first step. Guarded by a test in
`native/__tests__/legal.test.js`.

**Still yours to do:** the duty is operational, not just textual. When a
complaint arrives at your contact address you have 30 days to acknowledge it.

**Sources:** [DPA 2018 s.164A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/12/section/164A/2025-06-19?timeline=false&view=plain) · [DUAA 2025 s.103](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/section/103/data.html) · [SI 2026/82 reg. 7](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/82/regulation/7/made)

### 2. The sign-in link was carrying a working session through an unowned scheme

Found by working through the toolkit's secure-auth and deep-link checklist
items, and it is the only real security defect the review found.

The emailed sign-in link returns to `prepaconstable://auth-callback`. A custom
URL scheme has no owner: any other app on the phone may register the same one,
and iOS then chooses between them with no guarantee it chooses yours. Under the
implicit OAuth flow — which was in use — that link carries the access and refresh
tokens themselves, so whichever app caught it would hold a working session for
that account.

**Changed** in `native/src/cloud.js`: the Supabase client is now configured with
`flowType: 'pkce'`. The link carries a single-use code instead, which is useless
without the `code_verifier`, and the verifier never leaves this app's own
storage. `completeFromUrl` exchanges the code, and keeps the old token branch
only so a link already sitting in somebody's inbox still works. Five tests in
`native/__tests__/cloud-auth.test.js` cover it, including that an intercepted
code fails the exchange.

The complete fix is a Universal Link on a domain you control, which no other app
can claim. That needs the domain that will also host your privacy policy, so the
two jobs land together. Until then PKCE closes the leak.

### 3. Three places described the microphone, and one of them was still wrong

The privacy notice was corrected during the main audit to say that speech is
transcribed on the device where the device supports it and sent to Apple or
Google where it does not. The drill screen says the same and branches correctly
at runtime. Two other surfaces had not been brought into line:

- **`native/app.json`** — the iOS speech-recognition permission string ended
  "Nothing is recorded or uploaded." That sentence appears in the **system
  permission dialog**, which for most users is the first thing they ever read
  about the microphone, and on a device without offline transcription it is not
  true. **Changed** to name Apple's speech service and to say you never receive
  or keep the audio. Guarded by three new tests.
- **`docs/APP-STORE-LISTING.md`** — the Notes for Review told the reviewer "no
  audio is recorded, stored or transmitted", and the App Privacy section
  justified answering **No** to Audio Data on the same false basis. **Changed**
  both. The answer is still **No**, but for the correct reason: transcription runs
  through Apple's own `SFSpeechRecognizer`, so *you* never collect the audio,
  which is what Apple's question actually asks. That reasoning is now written
  down, including what to say if a reviewer queries it.

This is the failure mode the checklist's "privacy labels must match real data
behaviour" item exists to catch, and it was real.

### 4. A date correction to the main audit

`UK-COMPLIANCE-AUDIT.md` recorded the DMCCA 2024 subscription regime as
anticipated for spring 2027. The Government's announcement of 9 August 2026
brought it forward to **January 2027**. Corrected. It remains moot for a one-off
paid app — it would only bite if you ever moved to a subscription.

## Checked and already satisfied

From the toolkit's pre-submission checklist, the items this app already meets:

- Privacy manifest present and internally valid — `ios.privacyManifests` in
  `app.json` passes the toolkit's own `validate-privacy-manifest.py` with no
  findings, and nine dependencies ship their own, which Apple merges.
- In-app account deletion (Guideline 5.1.1(v)) — Profile → Delete account.
- No third-party social login, so no Sign in with Apple obligation.
- Both permission strings name the real feature rather than asking in the abstract.
- App name 16 characters, against a 30-character limit.
- No tracking, so no App Tracking Transparency prompt.
- Screenshots at 1320 × 2868, Apple's 6.9-inch size.
- Store billing only; no subscription, so none of the negative-option rules engage.
- Everything works without an account, so no demo account is needed for review —
  the Notes for Review say so explicitly.
- `ITSAppUsesNonExemptEncryption: false`, correct for an app whose only
  cryptography is standard HTTPS.

## Deliberately not done, with reasons

- **Session tokens remain in AsyncStorage rather than the iOS Keychain.** The
  checklist flags this and it is a fair point. It needs a chunking storage
  adapter and testing on a real device, which cannot be done from here. On iOS
  the app sandbox and full-device encryption mean this is a hardening step, not
  an exposure. Unchanged and still on the list — the PKCE change above reduces
  what an intercepted link is worth, which was the sharper end of the same risk.
- **AsyncStorage is not excluded from iCloud backup.** Same category. Worth
  doing alongside the Keychain move, not before.
- **No certificate pinning, jailbreak detection or Play Integrity.** These are
  the checklist's recommendations for apps holding money or health data. Pinning
  in particular has a real cost: it breaks the app when a certificate rotates.
  Disproportionate for revision progress, and none of it is required by UK law
  or by Apple.
- **Nothing was installed into `~/.claude/`**, as above.

## A note on what this document is

This is a record of checks run and changes made. It is **not** a statement that
the app is legally compliant or certified, and nobody should read it as one. It
describes compliance readiness and says plainly where the judgement calls are.
Item 8 above is not a formality.
